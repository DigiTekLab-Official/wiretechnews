Seven years after its entry into force, the General Data Protection Regulation remains the most consequential data protection law in the world — not because of its geographic scope alone, but because of the compliance infrastructure and organisational culture it requires organisations to build.

This piece covers the essential legal architecture compliance teams need to understand: the principles that govern all processing, the six lawful bases, the rights individuals hold, and the accountability obligations that regulators audit first.

## The Seven Principles in Practice

GDPR's seven principles (Article 5) are not aspirational — they are enforceable obligations. Enforcement decisions consistently cite principle violations as the root cause of fines, even when the immediate trigger was a breach or complaint.

**Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency** is often treated as the "lawful basis" principle alone, but fairness and transparency are independent obligations. Fairness means not using data in ways individuals would not reasonably expect; transparency means providing accurate, timely information in plain language through privacy notices and records of processing.

**Purpose limitation** has become one of the more litigated principles. Downstream uses of data — for analytics, profiling, product improvement, or sale — must be compatible with the original collection purpose. Regulators apply a compatibility test weighing the link between purposes, context of collection, nature of data, consequences for data subjects, and safeguards in place.

**Data minimisation** requires ongoing challenge of data collection decisions. The question is not "is this data useful?" but "is this data necessary for this specific purpose?"

**Accuracy** creates active obligations — particularly for systems processing data used to make decisions about individuals. Employee, customer, and patient records must have correction mechanisms that actually function.

**Storage limitation** is frequently deprioritised. Organisations with retention schedules that exist on paper but are not implemented technically are exposed. Automated deletion or anonymisation, with documented exceptions, is required.

**Integrity and confidentiality** means appropriate technical and organisational security measures — which requires a risk-based assessment, not a checkbox exercise. Appropriate measures for a healthcare data processor differ from those for a B2B SaaS company.

**Accountability** is the meta-principle. Article 5(2) requires controllers to be able to demonstrate compliance with all of the above. Documentation — records of processing activities, DPIAs, lawful basis assessments — is both the mechanism and the evidence.

## The Six Lawful Bases

Every processing activity requires a lawful basis identified before processing begins. The six bases under Article 6 are not interchangeable — selecting the right one has legal and procedural consequences.

**Consent** is often over-relied upon. Under the GDPR, consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous — demonstrated by a clear affirmative action. Pre-ticked boxes do not constitute consent. Withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent. Consent is appropriate for optional, non-essential processing where individuals have genuine choice; it is not appropriate where processing is a condition of service.

**Contractual necessity** covers processing required to perform a contract with the data subject, or to take steps at their request before entering into a contract. It applies to the actual processing required, not processing that is merely convenient or useful.

**Legal obligation** applies where processing is required to comply with EU or member state law. It cannot be self-referential — an internal policy does not constitute a legal obligation.

**Vital interests** is reserved for life-or-death situations and is rarely the appropriate basis for routine commercial processing.

**Public task** applies to public authorities and bodies exercising official authority. It is available to some private organisations carrying out tasks in the public interest under EU or member state law.

**Legitimate interests** is the most flexible basis and the most litigated. It requires a three-part test: (1) the interest must be legitimate; (2) processing must be necessary to achieve it; (3) the interest must not be overridden by the data subject's interests or fundamental rights. Controllers must document this balancing test. The basis is unavailable to public authorities for their official functions.

## Individual Rights Infrastructure

Compliance with individual rights obligations requires operational infrastructure, not just policy documents. Organisations must be able to:

- **Receive and identify** subject access requests (SARs) — including requests arriving through unusual channels
- **Search and retrieve** data across fragmented systems, including backups, archives, and third-party processors
- **Respond within one month**, with extension to three months only for complex or numerous requests
- **Provide information in a portable format** for data processed on the basis of consent or contract
- **Action erasure requests** technically — including cascading deletion requests to processors and sub-processors

Rights requests are the most common source of regulatory complaints. Under-resourcing the response function is a significant enforcement exposure.

## Accountability Infrastructure

Regulators expect to find the following in place during audits and following breaches:

**Records of processing activities (RoPA)** — required for organisations with 250+ employees, and de facto required for smaller organisations as the practical foundation for compliance. The RoPA maps all processing activities, their purposes, lawful bases, data categories, retention periods, and transfers.

**Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)** — mandatory for high-risk processing (large-scale processing of special category data, systematic monitoring, novel technologies). The DPIA process should be integrated into project delivery, not bolted on retrospectively.

**Data protection by design** — privacy controls must be embedded at the design stage of new systems and processes. The ICO and other DPAs expect to see evidence of early engagement with privacy requirements in project documentation.

**Processor agreements** — every processor handling personal data on behalf of the controller must have a written contract meeting Article 28 requirements. This includes cloud providers, marketing platforms, HR systems, and analytics tools.

**Breach response procedures** — the 72-hour notification clock starts when the controller becomes aware of a breach. Procedures must be operable at the speed the clock requires.

## Looking Forward

The GDPR's enforcement maturity has increased substantially since 2018. Supervisory authorities have issued billions of euros in fines, established interpretation precedents through the EDPB, and developed guidance that fills gaps in the regulation's text.

For compliance teams, the challenge is no longer primarily interpretive — the main pillars of GDPR compliance are well-understood. The challenge is operational: making privacy controls durable, auditable, and embedded in the systems and processes of organisations that change faster than their compliance documentation.
